The twenty-tens were the easy decade, and the nineties the next easiest: rates fell under buyers who already owned, and prices held near three and a half years of income. Sources

  • Mortgage rate. Freddie Mac, Primary Mortgage Market Survey: thirty-year fixed, weekly, averaged to each year.
  • Existing-home price. The National Association of Realtors' median existing-home price. Through the early twenty-tens, NAR's own annual figures, as HUD reprinted them in U.S. Housing Market Conditions (and a later edition). NAR does not publish its annual history freely after that, so later years are an estimate: the FHFA repeat-sales house price index, pinned to NAR at both ends — its last reprinted year and its latest months.
  • New-home price, for contrast. U.S. Census Bureau and HUD, median sales price of houses sold, mostly new construction.
  • Household income. U.S. Census Bureau, median household income (CPS ASEC, table H-5), current dollars.
  • One earner's pay. BLS, median usual weekly earnings of full-time wage and salary workers, times fifty-two.

What this leaves out

  • Where you live. These are national medians. In the most expensive metros prices run far higher against local incomes, and this report has no metro series to compare eras there.
  • Local taxes and insurance. Both are one national rate for every year, so no era gets a friendlier assumption. Real bills vary a lot by place; set yours in the what-if.
  • Refinancing costs are left out, which slightly flatters buyers who refinanced, the eighties buyer included. Mortgage insurance on a smaller down payment is left out too.
  • The future. Any buyer whose ten years run past the data gets projected years, on one stated assumption, and is marked projected everywhere. The projection is a what-if, not a forecast.

The method, in words

Each year's buyer puts a fifth down on the median existing home, pays closing costs, and takes a thirty-year fixed loan at that year's average rate. The monthly payment adds property tax and insurance, at the same national rates every year. Buyers are then followed for up to a decade: they refinance when rates fall a full point, their taxes grow with the home's value, and each year's payment is measured against that year's median income. Years past the data are projected: the rate stays at the latest year's average, and income and home values grow at income's pace over the last decade. The work is in transforms/affordability_transform.py; refresh the data from the publishers with python inputs/fetch_housing.py before citing a figure (inputs/housing_sources.md).
